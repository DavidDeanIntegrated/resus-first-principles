# V3-AC3 before/after quotes (0006-astra-p1.py, base 074a4a6)

## F04/F05

**Before:** In cardiac arrest the heart has stopped generating pressure, so every milliliter of coronary and cerebral blood flow has to be manufactured from outside the chest. It helps to keep three quantities apart. Pressure is what the compression produces in the aorta. A gradient is the difference between two pressures. Flow is what actually reaches tissue, and it follows the gradient, not the pressure alone.

**After:** In cardiac arrest the heart is no longer generating effective pressure, so nearly all coronary and cerebral blood flow during CPR depends on what compressions produce from outside the chest. It helps to keep four quantities apart. Pressure is what the compression produces in the aorta. A gradient is the difference between two pressures. Resistance is how much a vascular bed opposes flow. Flow is what actually reaches tissue, and it is set by the gradient divided by the resistance, not by the pressure alone. Resistance is why the same gradient can deliver different flow: coronary resistance is high while the compressed myocardium squeezes its own vessels and lower during release, and a vasoconstrictor such as epinephrine raises aortic pressure largely by raising peripheral resistance, which helps the heart and brain only if their own beds are not constricted to the same degree.

## F01

**Before:** Every positive-pressure breath is also a circulatory event. It raises intrathoracic pressure, and blood returns to the heart only when the pressure outside the chest exceeds the pressure inside it. Extra breaths narrow that difference, so venous return falls, and with less blood entering the heart, the compressions produce less coronary perfusion pressure.

**After:** Every positive-pressure breath is also a circulatory event. Venous return is driven by the difference between mean systemic filling pressure, the pressure the blood volume exerts on the stretched walls of the systemic veins, and right atrial pressure, and it is opposed by the resistance to venous return. A positive-pressure breath raises intrathoracic pressure, and with it right atrial pressure. If filling pressure and venous resistance stay roughly constant over that breath, the driving gradient narrows and venous return falls. More breaths, or breaths held longer, keep that gradient narrowed for more of each minute. With less blood entering the heart, each compression has less to eject, and coronary perfusion pressure tends to fall. The prediction weakens when those held-constant terms move: a volume-loaded or vasoconstricted patient has a higher filling pressure and tolerates the same breath better, while a hypovolemic patient loses venous return sooner.

## VENT

**Before:** Its limits are real. A low or falling value is a prompt to reassess compression quality and tube position. Do not use end-tidal CO2 alone to stop resuscitation. In an intubated adult, failure to exceed 10 mm Hg after 20 minutes of ALS may be one part of a multimodal decision to stop. A sudden rise can accompany ROSC, but there is no single cutoff that proves it.

**After:** Because the number has so many inputs, it is better used as a question than as a verdict. A low or falling value asks first whether the compressions have weakened or the tube has moved, and only later whether the patient can be resuscitated. Even then it should not decide the matter alone: in an intubated adult, failure to exceed 10 mm Hg after 20 minutes of ALS may be one part of a multimodal decision to stop. A sudden rise can accompany ROSC, though no single cutoff proves it, so the rise prompts a pulse check rather than replacing one.

## F06

**Before:** Epinephrine raises aortic pressure, which helps coronary perfusion, but it also redistributes flow so that less blood reaches the lungs for a short time. Less pulmonary blood flow carries less CO2 to be exhaled, so the reading dips while perfusion of the heart may be improving.

**After:** Epinephrine raises aortic pressure, which helps coronary perfusion, but a transient fall in end-tidal CO2 after a dose has been observed, and the proposed explanations, less CO2 delivered to ventilated lung or more ventilated lung that is poorly perfused, are not settled. Whatever the mechanism, the reading can dip while perfusion of the heart is unchanged or improving.

## F03

**Before:** That uncertainty is not a reason for emergency angiography: in a comatose patient without ST elevation, shock, or instability, emergency angiography has shown no benefit.

**After:** Persistent ST elevation with a suspected cardiac cause calls for emergency coronary angiography, whether or not the patient is comatose. Without ST elevation, emergency angiography is reasonable for selected patients with a suspected cardiac cause who have cardiogenic shock, recurrent ventricular arrhythmias, or evidence of significant ongoing myocardial ischemia, again regardless of coma. In a comatose patient with none of those features, no ST elevation, shock, electrical instability, or significant ongoing ischemia, emergency angiography is not recommended over a delayed or selective strategy, because randomized trials found no benefit from going early.
